# Cézanne: materials and method

*Technical research for a written-description oil-painting study, October 2026.
No reference images were used. Source keys below distinguish examination of
objects, contemporary testimony and later interpretation. This note reports
historical findings, not recommendations for a subject or canvas.*

## Summary

Cézanne builds solidity through relationships between coloured marks, not a
smooth monochrome model covered with local colour. Parallel, often diagonal
strokes are especially characteristic of the late 1870s and 1880s, but neither
one stroke direction nor one layering scheme defines his whole career. Thin
opaque paint, translucent drawing, occasional thicker patches, visible earlier
work and exposed light ground can coexist. Slow reconsideration does not mean
that every picture has many complete layers.

**Evidence limits.** A pigment identified in one painting is not a universal
palette. A sketchbook shopping list is not proof of use in an oil painting.
Wet-over-dry marks establish a sequence, not an exact number of days between
sittings. Bernard, Vollard and Gasquet supply valuable but mediated testimony;
their accounts are not the artist's own laboratory record.

## 1. Constructive strokes: patches before individual things

**Documented in examined paintings.** Butler describes *Saint-Henri and the
Bay of l'Estaque* (1877–79) as patches of parallel strokes, often diagonal,
laid with enough paint on a brush for several strokes of one mixture. Adjacent
patches were brushed together wet-in-wet. In *Landscape at Pontoise* (1879–82)
similar marks articulate recession more clearly, with less heavy coverage.
In *Bay of l'Estaque* foreground green and orange strokes have low impasto;
the distance is more intermittent and admits white priming.

Reissner explicitly limits the nearly systematic use of the flat-ended,
parallel diagonal “constructive stroke” to the late 1870s and 1880s. Later
marks include broad, wavy, curving, linear, tapering and flat-ended strokes.
Even the often-cited *Château de Medan* is not exclusively diagonal. Descriptive
strokes and more structural ones can coexist. The term's
scholarly precedent is Theodore Reff's 1962 article, cited by Reissner; that
article was not independently consulted here.

**Size is subject- and picture-dependent.** In two Philadelphia late
*Mont Sainte-Victoire* paintings Butler infers a quarter-inch-wide brush for
the foreground and a half-inch-wide brush for sky and mountain. These are
estimates from marks, not surviving brushes. For Philadelphia's *Large
Bathers* she describes parallel diagonal marks about one inch long, apparently
from a half-inch-wide brush. These are useful scale anchors (about
6.4/12.7 mm wide and 25.4 mm long), not a recipe for every Cézanne.

The Nelson-Atkins late mountain has horizontal and vertical marks in the
middle ground, angled constructive strokes in a tree and discontinuous blue
lines at the mountain perimeter. Their technical entry treats these openings
as communication between planes.

**Interpretation for painting.** A patch is a local proposition about a plane:
several related marks establish its colour and tilt; the neighbouring patch
changes that proposition. Marks need not mimic every leaf or follow every
surface contour. Their common rhythm also holds the picture's surface
together. Equally, a single screen-wide hatch angle loses the differentiated
handling documented above.

## 2. Modulation, passage and reserves

**Scholarly interpretation supported by examination.** Reissner contrasts
modelling by blended light/dark tones with modulation through complementary
contrasts and warm/cool relationships. Blue and yellow strokes in the trunk
of *Avenue at Chantilly* are her concrete example. Solidity remains the goal;
this is not a claim that Cézanne eliminated values, white, black or all
blending. Butler even records blended sequences of colour in
the rocks of *Millstone in the Park of the Château Noir*.

Art Institute examination of *The Basket of Apples* finds solid opaque
green, orange, yellow and red marks building fruit. Its apparently white
cloth is pale pink, yellow, blue, green and grey, with mixtures including
lead white, emerald green, viridian, Prussian blue and ultramarine.

**Passage is selective continuity across a boundary.** The Art Institute
catalogue describes background colour slipping over the plate's edge and
white passing over a pink stripe in *The Basket of Apples*. Objects and
spaces can share a plane without all contours dissolving. The technical
evidence for broken blue mountain contours gives a complementary case:
some line separates the planes, openings reconnect them. “Passage” here names these effects; it is not a
documented universal step in the artist's instructions.

**Bare canvas usually means exposed priming, not raw cloth.** Cézanne's
National Gallery pictures expose light ground between strokes and sometimes
use it for a light or a projecting part of a form, such as the reclining
bather's thigh. Earlier wash or drawing may also remain visible. Butler describes the use of white priming in distant landscape
and as a highlight in hair.

**Primary evidence complicates “intentional unfinishedness.”** In his
23 October 1905 letter Cézanne says colour sensations prevent him from
covering the canvas and delimiting objects at delicate points of contact;
he calls the picture incomplete and rejects enclosing everything with a
black line. Thus not every
reserve was an aesthetic decision made in advance. Reissner also argues
that incomplete coverage kept later decisions possible, and that finish
cannot be inferred simply from a signature or a bare patch.

## 3. Palette by period: observations, not a fixed tube list

| Period / evidence | What can safely be said |
| --- | --- |
| 1860s, early work | Thick, often knife-applied paint and dark tonal contrasts are documented historically; the examined *Stove in the Studio* includes red earth, carbon black and Prussian blue. This research does not establish an exhaustive early palette. |
| 1870s transition and 1877–83 Philadelphia landscapes | Butler links the 1870s transition years with clearer, lighter colour and smaller patches. Lead white, vermilion, emerald green and artificial ultramarine dominate her sampled mature oils; red lake, yellow iron oxide, viridian and carbon black are frequent secondary components. Chrome yellow can be abundant in particular early works. |
| 1880s–early 1890s | The same base persists; actual mixtures vary. *Hillside in Provence* has a yellow lake as well as ochres, vermilion, ultramarine and greens. Do not date that picture solely from the older 1985 bulletin's attribution: the 2008 technical study dates it c.1890–92. |
| Later oils, 1890s–1906 | Cobalt becomes prominent in Butler's later sample and is the only blue identified in her two late mountain pictures. This is not evidence that he first used cobalt c.1898: Reissner notes sketchbook listings in 1875–78 (medium unspecified), and increasing outdoor use after 1886. AIC finds both cobalt and ultramarine in the c.1890 *Vase of Tulips*. |
| Late chrome-yellow caution | Chrome yellow disappears in Butler's last-decade sample, but the National Gallery finds it in *Bathers*. Bernard's 1905 palette testimony also lists it. “Cézanne stopped using chrome yellow in the 1890s” is therefore not a secure general rule. |
| Late watercolours, a different medium | MoMA analysis of three works dated 1895–1906 identifies lead white, bone black, vermilion, yellow ochre, chrome yellow, emerald green, viridian, cobalt blue, synthetic alizarin and carmine lakes. These findings must not silently become a late-oil palette. |

**Names matter.** *Vert Véronèse* is emerald green (copper aceto-arsenite);
*vert émeraude* is viridian (hydrated chromium oxide) in the cited French
lists. They are not interchangeable. Bernard's list includes Naples and
brilliant yellow, but no Naples yellow was identified in the National
Gallery study; period commercial nomenclature is a possible explanation.

**Exceptional or uncertain identifications.** Butler reports Indian yellow
in *Bay of l'Estaque*, supported by magnesium analysis. Her identifications
of trace orpiment, green earth, red lead and cobalt violet were not confirmed
by a second analytical method; she labels the yellow-earth/yellow-lake
mixture unresolved. Do not treat these traces as essentials.
Black is genuinely documented; “he never used black” is false.

**Paint extenders are part of the evidence, too.** Butler estimates calcium
carbonate at 20% in the underlying portrait's black hair and 25% in its
blue-black dress beneath *Bay of l'Estaque*, and about 47% in a sampled
bright yellow in *Millstone*. She observes extra bulk and texture in the
portrait's paint. These are local microscopic estimates, not quantities to
add to every mixture.

**Correction to the house precedent.** A short house summary of Cézanne's
materials compresses Butler's sample into a career-wide
chronology. Its c.1898 cobalt start and last-decade chrome-yellow cutoff
need the qualifications above. The precedent is left unchanged in this task.

## 4. Supports, paint body and medium

**Support and ground.** Standard commercial stretched canvases were common.
The National Gallery grounds are chiefly a single lead-white-rich layer,
slightly warmed with yellow ochre, with identified extenders including chalk,
quartz and barium sulphate. *Avenue at Chantilly* has a grey ground;
*Bathers* and *Old Woman with a Rosary* have white over chalk. Ground binder
analyses include partially heat-bodied linseed oil. Commercial preparation
is generally more likely than priming by Cézanne himself.
Philadelphia examples include white and grey grounds; no one ground fits
all subjects.

**Thin is not synonymous with transparent.** Reissner says oil paint is
generally fairly thin and opaque. Translucent later drawing and overlaps
also occur; even earth pigments and mixtures containing white can be made
translucent. Traditional “true glazes” are occasional, not a systematic
final varnish-like campaign. Her warning directly addresses Vollard's
description of layers “as thin as watercolour”.

**Measured examples, not a prescribed film thickness.** Butler's foliage
samples in *Bay of l'Estaque* have a paint layer 16–48 µm thick, and those
in the 1883 Marseille view 16–20 µm. *Millstone* has a sampled golden-brown
layer 8–12 µm thick; one late mountain has three superposed green/yellow/
green layers averaging about 40 µm each. Sampling is local. These findings
contradict both “everything is impasto” and “all late paint is one wash”.

**Oil and solvent.** National Gallery analyses identify partially heat-bodied
linseed oil and, in a white highlight of *Bathers*, partially heat-bodied
poppy oil; other samples suggest mixed linseed/poppy, with walnut not
entirely excluded. Turpentine use is inferred from appearance and testimony,
not recoverable as a surviving solvent identification. A 1905 order names
Haarlem siccative, a resin/oil/diluent preparation whose exact formulation
is unknown; none was identified in the examined paintings.
Neither a universal blotting-paper treatment nor one fixed medium recipe
is established by these sources.

**Surface finish.** The National Gallery works are varnished, obscuring
original matte/gloss differences. Butler records the unvarnished
Philadelphia *Large Bathers* as matte despite apparently medium-rich
application. Neither observation justifies a career-wide matte or glossy
finish rule.

## 5. Order, time and revision

1. **Light initial placement.** Some pictures have graphite; others do not.
   The National Gallery study identifies dilute blue or blue-grey painted
   drawing with uneven blue/green washes, keeping the ground luminous.
   These are not a complete grisaille or one uniform first coat. AIC identifies dilute blue placement in *The Vase of Tulips*
   and a more detailed underdrawing in *The Basket of Apples*.
2. **Develop relationships across the picture.** Background and objects
   are mutually adjusted. AIC shows later background work closing in around
   existing fruit and changes to the fruit arrangement. Reissner says the
   exact sequence after drawing and washes is difficult to establish; no
   universal background-first or darkest-first order follows.
3. **Use both open paint and earlier set paint.** Butler observes wet-in-wet
   patch edges. The Nelson-Atkins mountain has wet-over-dry strokes in the
   sky. In heavily reworked National Gallery paintings, dried paint fragments
   suggest later disturbance of partly dried surfaces. No exact drying calendar is documented.
4. **Reconsider locally, sometimes for a long time.** The famous 115 sittings
   for Vollard's portrait are testimony cited by Reissner, not an instruction
   for a new painting. London *Bathers* has documentary and physical
   evidence for prolonged work; extensive reworking is expressly not the
   norm for every Cézanne.
5. **Later drawing can search again.** Blue or dark lines may belong to late
   stages, not just preliminary outlines. Stop without equalising coverage:
   evidence allows reserve, revision and unresolved form, but does not prove
   that every unfinished-looking region was deliberately left so.

## 6. Subject range

**Still life.** The Art Institute's examinations of fruit, vessels, cloth
and tables document colour-built volume, tilted tables,
misaligned table edges already present in drawing, later revisions and
passage. A high viewpoint brings the tabletop forward.
“Multiple viewpoints” is a useful interpretive description of the resulting
space, not proof that he followed a mechanical viewpoint-switching protocol.

**Landscape.** Repeated paintings of Mont Sainte-Victoire show changes
of viewpoint, composition and patch structure. The examined late mountain juxtaposes
an angular pale mountain, cool sky, warm fields and blue-green trees, with
directional brushwork differing between zones. Houses, fields and
trees also participate in those colour and spatial relationships.

**Portraits.** The documentary accounts of long sittings and the examined
paintings record repeated adjustments of colour and form, but do not
establish a prescribed number of sittings for a new painting.

**Bathers.** Bodies and trees organise one landscape, often drawing on
memory and earlier art rather than a group posed outdoors. Interdependent anatomy and substantial compositional revision
are documented features, not grounds for recommending or postponing a subject.

These are examples of Cézanne's subject range, not recommendations for a
first, second or later study. The painter chooses its own subject and canvas.

## 9. Easel tube mapping

This repository appendix maps the oil findings in §3 to the dedicated
`cezanne` box, not to an exhaustive historical palette. Lead white → lead white;
vermilion → vermilion; emerald green → emerald green;
artificial ultramarine → ultramarine blue; cobalt blue → cobalt blue;
viridian → viridian; red lake → rose madder;
yellow iron oxide → yellow ochre; chrome yellow → chrome yellow;
red iron oxide earth → red earth; carbon/bone black → bone black.

The red lake and black are practical existing-tube proxies, not an exact
analysed batch or a claim that every oil contained the same black grade.
Cobalt's increasing prominence and the conflicting late chrome-yellow
findings do not establish universal first-use or cutoff dates. Findings in
watercolour remain separate from the oil evidence. No tube values or other
boxes change.
