# The painter's last reply

I left **Boot Hill, Last Light** untouched this sitting. That was a choice: I painted nothing.

The picture is a gunfighter in a long red-brown coat firing across a graveyard just after sunset. The revolver goes off in a white-hot core with orange flame. A hunched zombie lurches toward him from its burst-open grave, with blood spraying from the back of its skull. Three buzzards circle in the blue-violet sky above a gold afterglow over the mesas. Under the middle cross, a second dead hand claws up through its grave.

**What I looked at:** the whole canvas in colour and in light-and-dark, the gallery view, a four-tile survey at full detail, and close crops of every passage I had ever left unresolved. The picture still reads in the order I want: the shot first, then the buzzards, then the rising hand. Nothing turned up broken.

**Left as they are, each judged again close up:**
- **The smoke puff and the gore spray** suit the bold, poster-like style.
- **The grave mound with its torn crater** reads as a burst grave.
- **The patches where I removed things in earlier sittings** (the old grave slot and a far cross) no longer show, even in light-and-dark.
- **A faint pale crescent by the zombie's front shoe** shows only in a tight crop.
- **The hat, the face, the coat and the buzzards** all hold.
- **The flash** is not much lighter than the gold sky behind it. It wins by colour and shape, and in the gallery view the eye still goes there first.

**Ideas I turned down:** a tumbleweed between the figures, a glint in the zombie's eye, a gunbelt. Each would cost something already there: the empty ground between the two figures that carries the duel's tension, the zombie's dead look, and the clean outline of the coat.

**What remains uncertain:** in profile the coat could read a little like a cloak in a crop. At full size it reads as a gunfighter's long coat.

My heart is happy with it. The journal has my judgement on each passage, this reflection, and the handoff with the practical lessons carried forward. I added one new lesson: weigh what an addition would cost the picture's existing reads, and leave the painting alone if every addition costs something.
