# Lawrence Alma-Tadema: materials and method

*A technical report from documented sources. "Uncertain" marks claims that are unverified,
disputed or taken from a single object. Most of the evidence is
documentary (his statements, witnesses, catalogs, supplier records),
with one instrumental examination of an early panel and conservators'
condition reports.*

## Summary

Alma-Tadema painted in oil on wooden panels and on canvas in roughly equal
numbers. Most of the panels are small, and the largest works are on canvas. He bought ready-prepared supports from London colormen and at
least once from a Brussels firm. The one
condition report that mentions his ground says it is white. He
sketched directly onto the support, laid in with rough brushstrokes and
developed passages in thin layers; unfinished work records different
stages of completion within a single canvas. From 1865 he painted with an oil copal varnish ("copal à
l'huile") mixed into the paint, having used boiled oil before that. A pupil published a palette attributed to him
around 1886 (§4). He reworked constantly, painting out
and scraping down finished passages, and this repainting is linked to
cracking in his paint.


**Terms.** *Ground/priming*: the preparatory layers between the support and
the paint. *Lay-in*: the first broad placing of shapes and colors.
*Painting out*: covering a finished passage with new paint. *Scraping
down*: removing paint with a knife or blade before repainting. *IRR
(infrared reflectography)*: imaging that shows carbon-based drawing under
paint. *Craquelure*: the network of cracks in paint or varnish. *Pentimento*:
an earlier stage that shows through paint laid over it. *Copal*:
a hard fossil resin, used in varnishes and media.

## 1. Supports

- **Panel and canvas.** The 1913 memorial exhibition catalog lists the
  support for about 140 works: about 73 panels and 70 canvases.
- **Sizes.** Panels are mostly small; several measure about 30 × 23 cm. A few are large: one of 1869 measures 28½ × 48 in.
  (72 × 122 cm), one of 1874 measures 26½ × 87½ in. (67 × 222 cm) and
  one of 1882 measures 86 × 26 in. (218 × 66 cm). The largest
  works are on canvas, for example 50 × 84 in. (127 × 213 cm), painted
  in 1888.
- **Bought prepared.** In answers to the Royal Academy's "Technical forms
  of procedure" questionnaires he recorded panels and canvas from one
  London colorman in 1873, 1877 and 1881, and a panel from the Brussels
  firm Mommen in 1877–78. A work of 1878
  and a small panel of 1889 bear the same colorman's marks; the 1889
  panel carries a "PREPARED PANEL" label. A panel of
  1909 and a work of 1902 carry labels of Roberson, another London
  colorman, with whom he had an account.
- **Wood species.** One panel of 1868–69 (72.5 × 109 cm) is cataloged as
  "oil on mahogany panel". An examined student-period panel (1860)
  is radially cut oak and was reused; IRR reveals an earlier painting
  underneath.
- **Panel construction.** A panel of 1867 has a join; a crack follows the
  join along its upper edge. *Single object.*
- **Canvas.** Canvases of 1861 (129.5 × 178 cm), about 1876 (89 × 53 cm)
  and 1904 (137 × 213 cm) have glue linings or linings, according to
  conservators' condition reports. One of his
  canvases in a London museum is still unlined.

## 2. Grounds

- **White.** A conservator's condition report on a panel of 1883 describes
  "the white ground revealed by the craquelure" and retouching laid in the
  cracks to hide it. *Single object.*
- **Commercial preparation.** Because he recorded ready-made colorman
  panels and canvases (§1), his grounds were usually commercial primings,
  not his own. *Inferred.*

## 3. Drawing and lay-in

- **On the support, not on paper.** In 1895 he wrote: "I generally make a
  slight sketch of the picture I am going to Paint directly on the
  respective canvas or panel". A biography of 1902 says he did
  little preparatory sketching: he "may be said not to sketch at all". The same author wrote in 1883 that "he never makes a sketch;
  his pictures are put upon the canvas at once".
- **Pencil.** In 1899 an interviewer saw a half-painted small canvas where
  "the paint and pencilled lines" both showed, and a large canvas "filled
  with pencilled outlines" for the next year's exhibition.
- **One early panel.** IRR of the 1860 panel revealed carbon-based
  underdrawing as well as the older painting beneath.
- **Brush lay-in.** An unfinished canvas of about 1873–75 records a rough
  brush lay-in, thin subsequent paint layers and passages at different
  stages of completion. He "set out the composition
  with rough brushstrokes" and then worked up the first passages one by
  one, while other elements were "still only a suggestion of their
  ultimate form".
- **Working from life.** He reported painting from live models and
  checking details against his collection of photographs, which he
  arranged by subject.

## 5. Media and varnish

- **Boiled oil, then oil copal.** In a letter of 1902 he wrote that a
  painting of 1864 was the last in which he used "boiled oil." A painting
  of 1862 made with "the same colours and oils" had been "full of cracks". On a visit to Paris in 1865 he asked a French
  painter what preparations he used and learned "to paint with Copal à
  l'huile: the varnish protects the colours against the influence of the
  atmosphere but when mixed with oil does not prevent the oil from
  evaporating. Since then I have always used this Copal à l'huile de
  Durosin". The article that publishes the
  letter reads "Durosin" as possibly a maker's name. A Paris pharmacist,
  Duroziez, made resin-based painting media that "lacked lead but still
  promoted drying by incorporating copal resin," with oil and turpentine; "Durosin" is probably his name misspelled. *Inferred.* Such
  copal media were valued for preventing "sinking in" and keeping the
  paint film flexible.
- **What copal à l'huile is.** An oil-based copal resin varnish.

## 6. Layers and application

- **Thin layers.** An unfinished work has thin paint layers. The
  early panel has thin wet-in-wet brush application and lead-white-rich
  paint of varying radiopacity; its density in X-rays suggests
  lead-white-rich paint, an inference, not a pigment analysis.
- **Order on the early panel.** Examination establishes successive
  applications, with accents added last. *Single early object.*
- **Passages at different stages.** In the unfinished canvas some
  passages are carried to finish while others remain lay-in.
- **Thick paint.** A panel of 1867 has localized thick impasto with
  craquelure. *Single object.* On a large canvas of 1904 a
  conservator found "a substantial and profound paint layer throughout"
  that is "not heavily textured," without thick impasto.
  *Single object.*
- **Brushwork.** A museum text describes fine brushstrokes.
- **Brushes.** He washed his own brushes.

## 7. Working sequence and reworking

- **Painting out and scraping down.** In 1899: "I never know how much or
  how little I am going to do. For days I make no progress with a
  picture—there is so much painting-out". His 1902
  biographer: "Again and again did he scrape down his work"; a
  1905 biographer lists "innumerable alterations, erasures, seemingly
  reckless scrapings down of canvas".
  "Some detail in a picture may be painted and repainted before it
  satisfies him". Witnesses describe repeated painting out
  and scraping down, including substantial changes to finished passages;
  a close friend wrote that one large canvas "represents the work of two
  or three pictures". A conservator notes
  that "as one very often finds with Alma-Tadema's work he has altered
  the composition in a few places"; on the 1904 canvas he
  painted over elements already laid in. One large canvas was
  probably altered after it was first exhibited in 1894.
- **Scraping to reuse.** The reused early panel was selectively scraped
  before repainting, "presumably to prevent the paint texture" of the
  earlier painting "from interfering".
- **Time per picture.** Reported working times range from nearly two
  years for one small canvas to one to three years for some works.
- **Several at once.** "I always have two or three pictures in hand at the
  same time… I must turn from one picture to another for quickness and
  freshness of interest". He left a picture upside down on
  the easel after a day's work on it.
- **Photographs of the work in progress.** In Brussels a photographer
  who lived in the same house photographed his paintings as they neared
  completion, and he used the prints "to correct compositional and tonal
  differences, in order to harmonise the various components of the
  painting". From 1866 he used the prints "to check the
  tonal balances," adjusted the painting and had it photographed again.
- **Working hours.** He worked every hour of daylight when at work on a
  picture.

## 8. Condition and aging

- **Cracking.** "Because his canvases were regularly painted over and worked
  up, they often exhibit a great deal of craquelure". He
  blamed the cracks in a work of 1862 on boiled oil and on its display
  conditions. On a panel of 1883 "deeper vertical craquelure"
  opens down to the white ground. On a panel of 1867 craquelure is
  in the thick impasto. On a canvas of 1861 his alterations "have
  given rise to some cracking in the dark colors".
- **Pentimenti.** On the 1904 canvas most retouches "only serve to
  diminish some original pentimenti," where his painted-over changes
  show through.
- **Panels.** The 1883 panel is "very stable with no warping"; the
  1867 panel "has not bowed".
- **Canvas.** An unlined canvas developed tears "along the fold line" after
  repeated loans.

## 9. The tubes here

The historical names in this note correspond to these tubes: flake
white → lead white; light red → red earth; cobalt → cobalt blue; ivory
black → bone black; Naples yellow → Naples yellow (lead antimonate). Pale
cadmium and viridian are in the manual's palette; his own use of them is
uncertain (§4).

## 10. At this easel

- **Support.** Plain-weave linen with ground layers laid from the tubes.
- **Paint.** Pigment from the tubes with an oil-medium share.
- **Taking paint off.** A brush picks up wet paint as it passes; that is
  how paint comes off the canvas.
- **Everything else** the easel does is in the easel guide.
