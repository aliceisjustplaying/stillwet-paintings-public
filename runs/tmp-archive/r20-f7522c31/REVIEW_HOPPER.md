# Edward Hopper materials note: contamination review

**Verdict: fix first.** The painter copy retains aesthetic judgments, visible research gaps, and claims whose excerpt-only provenance disappears when sources are stripped. The thirteen tube names match the proposed box, but two proxy mappings need explicit labels. No artwork title, depicted subject recipe, direct second-person advice, or literal name of another painter was found in the painter copy.

## Scope and standard

Reviewed `research/hopper_painter_copy.md`, `research/hopper_materials.md`, and `research/hopper_tubes.md`, using the materials-and-method boundary in `REVIEW_MATERIALS.md`, the round-2 corrections, and the accepted Sargent and Tonn notes. All line numbers below refer to **the painter copy**. Source keys identify evidence in the research version; that version and the tube proposal are outside the painter's reading boundary.

**Must fix** means direct aesthetic, subject, look, or identity contamination. **Should fix** means avoidable ambiguity, unnecessary visual association, missing qualification, or an unlabeled simulation substitution. **Fine** means legitimate materials, method, condition, or interface information. Historical claims are assessed against the supplied source records; this review does not independently authenticate the underlying publications or verify a running Hopper export. The American-city-life brief is an accepted experimental decision.

## Changes required

### 1. Must fix — aesthetic judgment attached to a scraped mark

**Lines 218–222:** “all scraped out—too heavy”; “he scraped off paint making a line of white”; “tricky & inconsistent”.

The anecdote supplies a white-line effect and an aesthetic rule against retaining it. “Tricky & inconsistent” has the same problem as the earlier Sargent edge-handling judgment. The paint-removal practice and concern about later show-through are useful technical facts.

**Rewrite:** “His wife recorded passages being scraped off and repainted. He expressed concern that covered paint might become visible through later layers as they aged.” Retain [LEV] in the research version and mark its excerpt provenance as described below.

### 2. Must fix — evaluative framing of direct painting

**Lines 184–185:** “I have a very simple method of painting. It's to paint directly on the canvas without any funny business”.

“Without any funny business” disparages other procedures and gives direct painting an aesthetic endorsement. The technical claim can stand by itself.

**Rewrite:** “He reported painting directly on the prepared canvas.” Retain the following object-specific wet-over-dry and cross-section evidence, with its access qualifications.

### 3. Should fix — visible gaps belong in research comments

**Lines 4–5:** “Little laboratory analysis of his oils has been published.” **Lines 61–62:** “No thread counts were found. *Gap.*” **Lines 85–86:** “No analysis of the ground's composition was found. *Gap.*” **Lines 122–123:** “No published laboratory pigment survey of his oils was found.” **Lines 200–203:** “Brush types and sizes are not documented”; “No surviving palette or account of his palette layout was found.”

These are research limitations that the assignment explicitly excludes from visible text. The opening publication-wide claim also exceeds a statement about what the researcher found.

**Rewrite:** Move each gap to a research comment. Begin the introduction: “The evidence assembled here comes mainly from record books, a 1959 interview, diary quotations, and museum technical notes.” Keep the positive weave description at lines 60–61, end the ground paragraph after the priming quotation, and delete the visible Brushes and Palette bullets. End the pigment introduction: “He reported using about twelve or thirteen colors.” Keep the research comment that he could not list them.

This does not require removing qualifications such as “Kind not stated” for black (line 137), uncertain pigment readings (lines 134–138), or an unidentified component of an observed faded mixture (lines 149–150). Those qualifications prevent false identifications.

### 4. Should fix — excerpt and summary access is only partly marked

**Lines 3–4:** “marks claims that are unverified, disputed or taken from a single object.” **Lines 97–99:** “worked through most of the compositional problems”; “only rarely show any alterations”. **Lines 186–187:** “shows eight or more layers”. **Lines 237–239:** “the flaking is extensive and old”; “zinc soaps are present”.

The research source register explicitly marks [LED] and [LEV] as Internet Archive search excerpts, [NGA-E] and [NGA-G] as search excerpts, [HUNT] and [YALE] as search summaries, and [IMA] as an encyclopedia summary. The painter copy preserves the [IMA] warning at lines 107 and 225, but omits comparable access labels from the quoted [YALE] and [HUNT] claims and the numerous ledger, diary, and museum claims. The generic definition of “Uncertain” does not identify which claims depend on restricted access. “Single account” identifies evidence scope, not access method.

**Rewrite:** Add to the introduction: “Much of the record-book evidence and the biographical evidence was […]” Add local labels to the affected passages, for example: “A conservation report describes eight or more layers in one sampled passage of a 1935 canvas. *Read through a search summary.*” At lines 97–99: “A museum report describes extensive preparation before painting and few alterations in X-rays of two canvases. *Read through a search summary.*” Apply the same label to the repeat at lines 223–224 and the 1935 condition findings at lines 237–239.

Preserve the distinction between full text and excerpts where [CGA] corroborates a ledger entry. The research register says [MORSE] was read in a full transcription; it should retain that status. Access provenance is already disclosed in the research register, so this finding concerns what survives into the painter copy.

### 5. Should fix — isolated evidence becomes a general working sequence

**Lines 18–20:** “The paint went on in successive layers, mostly wet over dry, over several weeks. Much of it is thin and opaque”. **Lines 207–211:** “Drawings on paper, then charcoal on the canvas, then blue paint over the charcoal lines”; “This order is assembled from several sources”.

The body assigns mostly wet-over-dry application to one canvas and the thin opaque layers to another. The summary broadens those findings to his oils generally. Section 7 makes blue over charcoal a regular step even though the summary correctly limits it to one recorded case. A warning about assembling sources does not fix that change in scope.

**Rewrite, summary:** “Examined canvases include successive layers, mostly wet over dry in […]”

**Rewrite, Order:** “Accounts describe preparatory drawings and drawing on the canvas; one […]” Preserve the access labels from finding 4.

### 6. Should fix — pigment use is tied to a desired color effect

**Line 128:** zinc white was used “to get a more brilliant and less warm white”.

This associates a pigment with a desired pictorial effect. Its use, date, binder, and subsequent replacement supply the relevant material evidence.

**Rewrite the evidence cell:** “Early record-book entries pair zinc white with poppy oil; it […]” Keep “Given up for lead white” as a dated practice fact, with the source qualifications.

### 7. Should fix — unnecessary evaluative wording

**Lines 43–44:** “the best [London colorman's] linen I can acquire”. **Lines 159–160:** “the most satisfactory mediums”. **Lines 197–199:** “a technique the cataloguers call ‘somewhat unusual’”.

These judgments add no measurable property or procedure. The first two concern material preferences, so their severity is lower than the aesthetic rules in findings 1–2.

**Rewrites:** “In 1959 he reported buying linen from a London colorman.” At lines 157–160: “In 1959 he reported discontinuing poppy oil because of concerns about cracking and using linseed oil and lead white.” At lines 197–199: “On one canvas of 1939, fine lines were drawn with graphite pencil over the paint. *Read through search excerpts.*” Keep the later dated oil records, which document a change in practice.

### 8. Should fix — unnecessary artwork-location clue

**Lines 235–237:** “On one canvas that came back from a museum in Chicago”.

The city and museum clue contributes nothing to the zinc-white condition evidence and narrows the identity of the work. This is an avoidable identification cue; the passage itself does not describe a recognizable scene.

**Rewrite:** “He believed zinc white could scale and crack, and thought a zinc-white passage in one returned canvas had required restoration.” Preserve the separate 1935 examination and its provenance label.

### 9. Should fix — tube proxies are presented as equivalences

**Lines 255–262:** “The historical names in this note correspond to these tubes”; “light red → red earth”; “black → bone black”; “Burnt sienna and ‘cadmium deep’ are uncertain readings”.

All thirteen destinations match the main proposed box: lead white, zinc white, yellow ochre, pale cadmium, cadmium yellow, deep cadmium, red earth, burnt sienna, cerulean blue, cobalt blue, ultramarine blue, viridian, and bone black. There is no missing or extra tube. The proposal explicitly calls red earth and bone black proxies; §9 drops that distinction. The black entry at line 137 leaves its kind unidentified. The proposed deep-cadmium hue is also an inference, beyond the uncertainty of reading the historical term.

**Rewrite:** Introduce the list with “The historical names are mapped to the supplied tubes as follows.” Change the relevant entries to “light red → red earth (proxy); unspecified black → bone black (proxy); ‘cadmium deep’ → deep cadmium (modeled as deep yellow-orange; inferred).” Retain the burnt-sienna uncertainty. If the experimenter adopts the proposal's optional light-red tube, change the corresponding mapping to “light red → light red” when assembling the export. The present red-earth mapping agrees with the main proposal.
